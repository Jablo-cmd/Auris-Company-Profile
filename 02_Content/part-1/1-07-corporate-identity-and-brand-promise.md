# 1.7 Corporate identity and brand promise

**Standfirst**

> What the name means, what the mark is built from, and the single commitment the company is
> prepared to be measured against.

---

## Copy

### The name

**Auris** is Latin for *the ear*. It denotes listening — receiving before responding, which is the
discipline our work begins with and the one most often skipped.

**Nexus** is Latin for *a binding, a connection*. It denotes the act of joining things that were
separate into something that holds. That is the substance of systems work: the value is rarely in any
single component, but in whether the connections between them are sound.

Read together the name describes a sequence rather than two attributes — **listen, then connect**.
The order is the point.

### The mark

The symbol is a stylised **A**: a solid apex rising at a constant three-to-five slope, encircled by
an orbital arc that resolves from cyan into navy.

**The apex** is stability and ascent — weighted at the base, directional at the top. It is drawn at a
single unvarying angle, and that angle now governs the geometry of everything in this publication:
the section markers, the list bullets, the card corners and the diagram connectors are all built on
it.

**The orbit** is the connective act the name describes. It does not enclose the apex or decorate it;
it binds it. In this document the orbit's colour gradient has been unrolled across the seven parts to
form the navigation system — so the progression a reader sees on the edge of these pages is the
mark's own gradient, expressed at book scale.

### The tagline

> **Connecting Innovation. Delivering Impact.**

Stated as a sequence, deliberately. Innovation that does not reach production is not innovation; it
is an expense. The connection has to be made before impact can be claimed, and most technology
failures occur in the gap between the two.

### Our brand promise

> **Technology built around the way your organisation works.**

Auris Nexus seeks to understand the underlying business or operational problem before recommending
technology, delivering solutions that are practical, scalable and appropriate to the organisation's
needs. That order — understanding, then recommending — is the promise, and it excludes a specific
failure mode: the vendor who has already decided which product to sell before the discovery meeting
ends.

Everything else in this publication — the delivery lifecycle from page 74, the way each service in
Part Two is scoped to what it actually solves, and the honest account of what has and has not yet been
built in Part Seven — exists to make that promise keepable. A promise a company has not organised
itself around is a slogan.
